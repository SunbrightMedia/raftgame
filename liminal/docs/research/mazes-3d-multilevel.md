# 3D and multi-level maze design

**Overview.** A vertical maze is hard to read because the player's mental map is usually a 2D plan. Games that do it well (Dark Souls, Metroid Prime, Piranesi's *Carceri*, parking structures) give players a few strong vertical anchors, show them places they cannot yet reach, and make every vertical link either reversible or an obvious one-way commitment. Games that do it badly (Descent's tunnel mazes, Minecraft mineshafts) lean on an automap or on the player's patience. For our industrial megastructure the lesson is to generate a connected multi-level graph first, then dress it so that sight lines, not just connections, carry the structure.

## 1. What the sources and precedents show

**Descent (1995).** **[verified]** Levels are built from cubes joined by portals, forming rooms and tunnels. Early levels were simple connected tunnels; as levels became confusing the developers added an automap (Wikipedia mirror; Olaf Eichler's "How to write a Descent level"). Fans describe D2 as more vertical and less tunnel-like than D1. **[memory]** With six degrees of freedom and no fixed "up", the main orientation problem is roll and pitch, not topology; levels that keep one dominant axis read better. Lesson: a 3D maze of identical tunnels forces a map. Ours has gravity and a fixed up, which removes most of that problem, so we should not need an automap if landmarks are good.

**Dark Souls.** **[memory]** Lordran is a single continuous 3D space in which areas overlap vertically (Firelink Shrine looks down on the Undead Burg and up at Anor Londo; Blighttown's bottom is visible from far above). The key techniques are: (a) a hub with few strong landmarks visible from many places; (b) one-way entrances (drops, elevators, doors that open only from one side) that later loop back as shortcuts; (c) sight lines that promise a place well before the route to it is found. Shortcuts are typically a ladder, lever or door that connects a far point back to a known point, so the player experiences the world as folded rather than long.

**Metroid Prime and lock-and-key.** **[verified, general]** Lock-and-key structure (barrier, then item, then barrier opened) is documented by Boris the Brave ("Lock and Key Dungeons") and Joris Dormans' mission-graph work (generate a graph of dependencies, then a separate step lays it out in space; cyclic generation as used in *Unexplored* nests cycles to avoid tree-shaped levels). **[memory]** Metroid Prime shows locked areas through glass, over gaps, and across rooms long before the ability to reach them; later rooms re-view earlier ones from new angles. A newer Prime game's review notes platforms that overlook an area being visible from that area later **[verified via a review snippet, weak]**.

**Minecraft strongholds and mineshafts.** **[memory]** Strongholds are a graph of rooms grown from a start piece, with stair rooms, library, portal room and dead ends; mineshafts are long corridors with crossings, vertical shafts and branching, which are easy to get lost in because everything is the same width and colour. Both are generated by recursive piece placement with a bounding-box check against earlier pieces. This is a useful baseline algorithm and a cautionary example of sameness.

**Parking structures.** **[memory]** Split-level or helical ramps make every floor look alike, so people lose track of level; real garages use floor colour and numbers. As liminal space they are good: a repetitive stack with ramps whose slope you feel in your legs, and sightlines through the open edge to other floors. The disorientation comes from "which floor am I on?", which we can exploit on purpose, with signage that is subtly wrong.

**Piranesi's *Carceri d'Invenzione*.** **[memory]** Staircases and bridges overlap in impossible ways; the eye cannot resolve which structure is in front of which. Game translation: many stairs and catwalks seen at once at different depths, arches, a few bridges that appear to lead somewhere but do not, and scale cues (tiny figures) that make the space feel huge. Susanna Clarke's *Piranesi* and House of Leaves use the same idea: an interior larger than its container.

**Weave mazes.** **[memory]** A weave maze lets passages cross over and under one another (the classic "over/under" corridor in Jamis Buck's maze writing). On a grid it is a normal maze plus bridge cells where a straight corridor passes over a perpendicular one; the connectivity graph is non-planar. This is a cheap way to produce the "I walked under the place I was just on" feeling.

## 2. Mechanisms for vertical links

- **Stairs**: reversible, readable, but they consume length (rise 4 m at about 35 degrees is about 6 m run plus landings). **[memory]**
- **Ramps**: slower and longer; good for vehicles and parking-style "this level is a spiral".
- **Ladders and tubes**: short footprint, usually connect through a hatch; good for tight, closed sections.
- **Lifts**: allow big height changes, double as a timed/gated link, and can be one-way if the call button is on one side.
- **Drops**: one-way; best when the landing already shows the return route (a visible ladder or lift) within a few seconds.
- **Broken or diagonal catwalks and fallen girders**: chaos links; they connect levels at odd angles and make the vertical structure feel damaged rather than designed.

## 3. Orientation without annoyance

**[memory/opinion]**
1. Give each vertical region one landmark visible from most of it (a huge vertical shaft, a lit tower, a hanging machine) and keep it consistent so players can triangulate.
2. Prefer *reversible* links; make one-way links rare and always show the way back within sight or a short walk.
3. Let players see through floors (grating, open shafts, glass) so that levels above and below act as context; keep that view partial (a slice through a shaft or a few grates) so that it hints and does not give a map.
4. Avoid a corridor that visibly leads to where you already were while being unreachable from there without a long detour more than a couple of times; it is a good trick once or twice and a chore beyond that.
5. Hold plan-view overlap to a controlled set of places; two stacked identical rooms with identical lighting make players think they have not moved.
6. Disorientation is fine when it is *temporary and recoverable*: a shaft of daylight, a unique sound, or a recognisable machine fixes it.

## 4. Generation on an irregular graph

**[memory plus Dormans/Boris as cited]**
1. **Mission graph first.** Create a spanning structure, then add 2 to 4 cycles (Dormans, cyclic generation) so loops exist by construction. Mark some edges as locks (door, power, missing bridge) and place keys earlier in the graph.
2. **Assign each node a height** from a small set of levels (for us 5 catwalk levels). Run a layered embedding: nodes are 2D points (irregular, e.g. from Poisson-disc or the existing mass layout) with an integer floor.
3. **Edges between different floors are vertical links.** Choose the link type by height difference and available footprint (stairs for 1 floor, ladder or lift for 2+, drop for one-way).
4. **Check feasibility** with swept volumes: a stair needs a clear box; a catwalk needs clearance above and below. Reject or re-route on collision (Minecraft-style bounding-box checks).
5. **Add weave crossings** where two edges cross in plan but at different heights; deliberately keep a few.
6. **Verify connectivity** with BFS over a graph where drops are directed edges; require that the start can reach every non-secret node, and that every directed edge has a return path within K steps.
7. **Metrics to test**: loop count, mean/max shortest-path length between random node pairs, dead-end fraction (roughly 10 to 25 percent, **my number**), floors visited per path, and a "sameness" score (adjacent nodes should differ in at least two of: height, enclosure, width, light, dominant axis).

## What this means for our game

Rules for the generator:
1. Build a graph with cycles first (spanning tree plus 2 to 4 loops per region), then embed it with floors; do not carve a grid maze and hope.
2. Give every node an enclosure tag (open hall, canyon, tight tube, gantry) and forbid two adjacent nodes from sharing the same tag.
3. Make most vertical links reversible; allow a small share (about 10 to 15 percent, my number) of directed drops, each paired with a visible return route.
4. Place at least one unreachable-but-visible target per region (an upper catwalk, a lit door across a gap) and make it reachable later by a loop.
5. Add 3 to 6 weave crossings per region where one route runs over another; make the lower one visible through grating.
6. Add shortcut edges that connect a far node back to a known landmark and require an action (lever, hatch) on the near side.
7. Add a "wrong link" budget: one or two links per region that break the rules (a stair that returns to the floor you left, a diagonal catwalk, a ladder that ends at a wall), always safe and never blocking progress.
8. Make vertical link type depend on height difference and footprint, and always check clearance volumes.
9. Place one dominant vertical landmark per region, visible from at least half of it.
10. Never repeat the same link geometry twice in a row along a path.

Layout and pacing:
- Alternate tight closed parts (ladder tubes, service crawls) with large open vertical halls; after a tight stretch, release the player into a space where levels above and below are visible.
- Reveal the vertical structure gradually: first see the tower from far away, then glimpse its lower floors through grating, then enter at a middle level.
- Use signs of wrongness sparingly: floor numbers that skip or repeat, a stair whose handrail is on the wrong side, the same corridor segment repeated N times with a tiny change, lights that are on only below you.
- Avoid: long identical stairwells without a landmark, unmarked one-way drops into dead ends, vertical overlap so exact that the player cannot tell floors apart, and any softlock.
