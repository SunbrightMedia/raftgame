# SCP-3001 "Red Reality": research for liminal

## Overview

SCP-3001 is a short epistolary horror piece. A researcher, Dr. Robert Scranton, is thrown by a lab accident into a "non-dimension" with almost no reality in it. His only companion is one object, a control panel with a blinking red light, and the article is essentially the audio logs he records on it. It has almost no setting, because the setting is almost nothing. What it has is a clock (his body and mind come apart over about six years), a single anchor (the panel), and a gradient of "how real is anything here". Those three things are the transferable part. A walking game cannot use diary entries as its main carrier (the brief says not text logs), but it can convert each device into space, light, geometry and scale.

## Findings

Verified against the article (scp-wiki.wikidot.com/scp-3001, fetched via summary tool); my own reading is flagged.

### The space
- The place is a "non-dimension" with Hume level 0.032 (Hume = a measure of local reality strength; normal is about 1.0). It contradicts the in-universe "Kejel's Laws of Reality". Matter inside decays toward that level.
- Reached by a "Broken Entry" wormhole after seismic damage to Lang-Scranton Stabilizer units in Reality Lab A, 2 Jan 2000.
- Scranton describes moving as swimming through thick, form-fitting black gel; he can travel in a plane and sink "down". He travels one direction for months and finds no end; he later estimates roughly 600 km of descent with a much longer climb back. Late in the logs an encroaching border appears about 3 km away, closing to 2. (Verified from the summary; I have not checked exact figures against the primary text, so treat the numbers as approximate.)
- Key technique (my reading): the space is defined by what is absent. Scale is conveyed only through his travel durations and distances, never by describing anything he sees. Emptiness is made frightening by making it unmeasurable and then, near the end, measurable and shrinking.

### Decay over time
- Hunger and thirst fade early; he realises he does not need food. Safety is removed first, so the horror is not "will I die" but "what is left of me".
- Middle: tingling and itching as his Hume field diffuses. Hands pass through each other; a thumb and a pinky detach, with no bleeding at first.
- Late: he has "static" qualities, passes through his own body, loses speech control and sounds childlike. Near the end perhaps a foot, some leg muscle, maybe a heart and a lung remain. He reports 184 failed suicide attempts, each leaving less of him.
- Simulation figure in the article: an organism can lose over 70% of tissue and still function if at least 40% of the brain remains. (Verified.)
- Technique: decay is staged and reported as a monotone ladder with strict ordering (extremities first, then continuity of self, then voice). The reader can always tell how far along they are.

### The single anchoring object
- The panel is the only thing that came with him and the only thing that records him. It "answers" nothing the way he wants, yet it is the reason the story exists (the logs).
- The red light is a recording indicator. He names it "red", talks to it, then suspects it is solid, that it hurts him, and that it might be "not human". The article never resolves this. (Verified.)
- Technique: one object in a void becomes a character. Its blink is the only rhythm in the world. Ambiguity about whether it is helping or harming is more durable than any explanation.

### Time, structure, and how the horror is paced
- Five log sections of rising degradation: orientation and panic (day 1 to about 11, he does not notice the recorder), physics of the place, despair at six months, bodily disintegration, final hum waves. Time stamps are given as elapsed durations, which makes the passage of time legible without a calendar.
- Hum pulses rise from 20 to 60 per minute and begin to hurt him; the panel, the sole detector, cannot register the hum. (Verified; the panel's blindness is a footnote.)
- The last log ends mid-call for his wife; the panel reappears at Site-120 in December 2005 covered in blood, with remains and his ring. The only "outside" viewpoint arrives after everything is over. (Verified.)
- Technique (my reading): the personal stakes (wife Anna, a never-born child's possible names) are told in the same register as the physics, so nothing is exposition. The setting is borrowed from the reader's own memory of loss.

### What I could not verify
Web search returned mostly fan blogs. I found no primary analysis of the article's structure beyond the article itself, so the "technique" notes above are my own reading.

## Spatial and environmental translation (own reading)

| Article device | Walking-game device |
|---|---|
| Hume level / reality thinning | A per-area scalar `realness` in [0,1] that drives geometry density, texture fidelity, lighting, audio |
| Decay toward the local level | Meshes lose detail with exposure time or depth: fewer polygons, flat colour, missing back faces, collision becoming soft |
| Almost-empty void | Large, near-empty volumes with one object and a horizon; measured by walking time, not by dressing |
| Single anchor | One lit object (a terminal, a lamp, a valve wheel) per thin zone that the player returns to |
| Red blink | One slow periodic light, never synchronised with anything else |
| Body decay ladder | Ordered staging of how a zone degrades, so players learn to read the stage |
| Border closing in | The walkable volume shrinks every visit |
| Elapsed-time stamps | Props show age in layers (dust, rust, clean outline of a removed object) rather than text |

## What this means for our game

Rules for the generator:
1. Give every region a `realness` value and make every visual and physical property read from it, so "thin" is a gradient and not a binary room type.
2. Thin zones lose things in a fixed order: small clutter, then surface detail and texture, then back faces and trim, then structure, then collision. Players learn the order and read progress from it.
3. Keep thin zones rare and place at most one per branch of the maze; each should hold exactly one anchor object and nothing else that is lit.
4. Make the anchor non-functional or ambiguously functional (a panel whose lights blink but whose switches do nothing), and never explain it.
5. Use one slow blink, around 2 to 4 seconds, in a single warm-red hue reserved for anchors; do not reuse that colour anywhere else.
6. Make void scale larger than the player can cross comfortably (60 to 150 m of nearly empty floor at walking speed) so crossing is itself the event.
7. Let the walkable area contract across revisits: the same hall has a wall, ceiling or floor edge nearer each time the player returns, never while they watch.
8. Decay should be monotone: a place that has decayed never recovers, and the seed fixes how far it has gone, so the same seed is the same ruin.
9. Prefer subtraction to addition. Remove a rail, a stair tread, a light, a floor tile; do not add monsters, text or jump scares.
10. Pair thin zones with dense, well-built zones directly before and after; the contrast is what makes the emptiness read.

Pacing:
- Introduce the first thin zone as a small anomaly (one missing wall panel), not a hall, so the player learns the language before the large version.
- Escalate by distance from the start: later thin zones are bigger and further along the ladder.
- Allow long stretches (several minutes) with no new object; silence is the pacing tool.

Signs of wrongness that are cheap to build:
- Textures lose resolution or palette steps toward a thin zone; shadows stop matching their caster.
- Geometry that partly overlaps itself (two stair flights occupying the same space, one rendered wireframe-flat).
- Floor that continues but with no collision, so the player slowly sinks (the "swimming" feel) over a fraction of a second.
- Audio: a low pulse whose rate rises with proximity (a nod to the 20 to 60 per minute hum), inaudible to the player's own HUD or meters.
- Props with a clean outline of what used to be there.

Avoid:
- Text logs, voice diaries or in-world explanations of the cause.
- A binary "glitch room"; it reads as a set piece and not as the world breaking.
- Reusing the anchor in every room; it must stay singular.
- Using the decay as a fail state or timer; the source's horror is being unable to stop it, not racing it.
- Hard resets that restore the world; permanence is the point.

## Lore tie-in (our world)
The industrial world is "breaking". A thin zone is a place where the structure has lost enough reality that its girders and walls are merging with the void: a catwalk ends in a flat grey plane, pipes taper to nothing, a lone control console blinks red. Entrances to other worlds can be placed in thin zones, since that is where the fabric is weakest.
